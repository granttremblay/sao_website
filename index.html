Serving the nation and the world

Our National & Global Impact

From planetary defense and frontier AI to pursuit of the greatest cosmic discoveries in history, our work serves the nation and the world. We are core to the Smithsonian's mission to not only diffuse human knowledge, but increase it.

We build the instruments that keep us at the forefront of ground-based astronomy, from a Giant Magellan Telescope spectrograph designed to search for life on other worlds — part of a 25-meter observatory rising in Chile — to world-leading black hole imaging developed through a global observatory network. This work is backed by nearly $1 billion in private investment across 36 states and delivered on the nation's schedule. The observatories behind it sustain skilled American jobs and serve researchers, educators, and industry in all 50 states.

  • ↗ Giant Magellan Telescope We're building critical GMT instrumentation, including a spectrograph to search for life on other worlds, for what will become the most powerful telescope in history.
  • ↗ Event Horizon Telescope We lead a global collaboration in black-hole imaging, with technology that also supports communications and national security applications.
  • ↗ Submillimeter Array We operate the SMA atop Maunakea in partnership with Taiwan's Academia Sinica, serving astronomers worldwide.
  • ↗ Whipple Observatory Home to VERITAS and the MMT, infrastructure serving researchers, educators, and industry.

We run planetary defense capabilities that no other institution hosts. Our Minor Planet Center is the world's sole authority for tracking asteroids and comets, feeding data to NASA's Planetary Defense Coordination Office and space-safety agencies worldwide. Its HITRAN database also underpins atmospheric research and national-security applications. We also help forecast space weather — the solar storms that can disrupt satellites, power grids, and communications — with instruments flying aboard NASA's Parker Solar Probe.

  • ↗ Minor Planet Center Our Minor Planet Center is the world's sole authority for tracking asteroids and comets, including hazardous near-Earth objects, and the foundational data layer for NORAD, the U.S. Space Force, and NASA's Planetary Defense Coordination Office.
  • ↗ TEMPO Satellite Our TEMPO satellite revolutionizes air quality monitoring by tracking air quality across North America in real time, neighborhood by neighborhood, every single hour of the day.
  • ↗ Parker Solar Probe Our instruments aboard NASA's Parker Solar Probe fly through the Sun's outer atmosphere, sampling the solar wind at its source. Understanding how the Sun drives that wind sharpens forecasts of space weather — the solar storms that can disrupt satellites, GPS, power grids, and communications here on Earth.
  • ↗ HITRAN Database Our molecular spectroscopy database underpins atmospheric and climate modeling worldwide, used across research, industry, and national security applications.
  • ↗ Greenland Telescope Our northernmost telescope, hosted at Pituffik Space Base in Greenland and built in partnership with Academia Sinica (Taiwan).

We apply frontier AI to scientific data at (literally) universe-spanning scale. We operate NASA SciX and the Astrophysics Data System, the discovery engine for the world's scientific literature used by more than 16 million people a year. By publishing our data and code openly, we set the standard for reproducible, accountable science.

  • ↗ AstroAI We apply frontier AI to astrophysical data at scale, already partnering with the Department of Energy and NVIDIA on AI-for-science.
  • ↗ NASA SciX & ADS Our NASA SciX and Astrophysics Data System (ADS) form the discovery engine for the world's scientific literature, serving 16+ million visitors a year.

We are the birthplace and national home of high-energy astrophysics, operating NASA's flagship Chandra X-ray Observatory for the entire U.S. astronomy community. This work earned the 2002 Nobel Prize in Physics and keeps us at the frontier of a field we created. We're also designing the X-ray observatories that come next, building on decades of leadership in the field.

  • ↗ Chandra X-ray Observatory We are the birthplace and national heart of high-energy astrophysics, operating NASA's flagship X-ray telescope since 1999, work recognized with the 2002 Nobel Prize.
  • ↗ Chandra X-ray Center Home to the science and flight operations that turn Chandra's data into discovery for the entire U.S. astronomy community.
  • ↗ Next-generation X-ray missions From the Einstein Observatory to Chandra to concepts like Lynx, we build the X-ray observatories that come next.

Our Central Engineering team builds instruments for missions no one else can deliver, from the Parker Solar Probe to the Giant Magellan Telescope's planet-hunting spectrograph. By supporting many missions at once instead of fragmenting work across single-project contracts, we stretch every federal dollar. This shared capacity is how we field cutting-edge instrumentation on schedule and on budget for the nation.

  • Central Engineering ↗ Our full-service engineering organization designs and builds state-of-the-art instrumentation for ground- and space-based missions, from the Parker Solar Probe to the Giant Magellan Telescope's planet-hunting spectrograph, supporting many missions at once rather than fragmenting capabilities across project-specific contracts.
  • Telescope Data Center ↗ Our Telescope Data Center coordinates the use of visible-light telescopes by astronomers, keeps archives of the data from these observations, and provides computer tools for processing the data.
  • Radio Telescope Data Center ↗ Our Radio Telescope Data Center (RTDC) is an archive of data collected by radio telescopes operated by the Center for Astrophysics, along with the computers and custom software packages used to process this data.

  • Black holes

    Humanity's first image of a black hole

    We led the Event Horizon Telescope's unveiling of the glowing ring of M87* in 2019, then our galaxy's Sagittarius A* in 2022, turning an untestable idea into an observable object.

    Image: Event Horizon Telescope Collaboration
  • Black holes

    The closest black hole to Earth

    Astronomers found a stellar-mass black hole locked in a quiet orbit with a Sun-like star just 1,560 light-years away, the nearest ever confirmed, hiding in plain sight.

    Illustration: International Gemini Observatory/NOIRLab/NSF/AURA

  • Dark matter

    Direct evidence for dark matter

    Chandra's image of the Bullet Cluster caught dark matter sailing ahead of colliding gas, the first direct empirical proof that it exists.

    X-ray: NASA/CXC/CfA · lensing & optical: NASA/STScI; Magellan

  • Cosmic structure

    The cosmic web

    Our CfA Redshift Survey produced the first true maps of large-scale structure, discovering the "Great Wall" of galaxies and revealing a universe of filaments and voids.

    Visualization: cosmological simulation
  • Exoplanets

    First evidence of worlds beyond our solar system

    Our researchers played a major role in the earliest observations providing convincing evidence of planets orbiting stars other than our Sun, opening the modern search for other worlds.

    Illustration: ESO
  • Exoplanets

    A volcanic world 90 light-years away

    Astronomers discovered LP 791-18 d, a rocky, Earth-sized planet that may undergo volcanic outbursts as extreme as Jupiter's moon Io, and that could still support life.

    Illustration: NASA/Goddard Space Flight Center/Chris Smith (KBRwyle)
  • Runaway stars

    A star flung out at 1.5 million miles an hour

    Our astronomers tracked a hypervelocity star speeding away from the galactic center, flung outward by a close brush with the Milky Way's central supermassive black hole.

    Illustration: ESA
  • Comets

    Comets are icy worlds

    Our first director, Fred Whipple, proposed in 1950 that comets are "dirty snowballs" of ice, rock, and dust, a theory confirmed decades later by spacecraft flying through a comet's own tail.

    Image: ESA/Giotto, comet Halley nucleus
  • Solar science

    Touching the Sun

    Our instruments aboard Parker Solar Probe sampled the solar wind as the spacecraft crossed into the Sun's corona in 2021, the first time humanity touched a star.

    Illustration: NASA/Johns Hopkins APL
  • X-ray astronomy

    Opening the X-ray universe

    From the Einstein Observatory to Chandra, our scientists built the field of X-ray astronomy, recognized with the 2002 Nobel Prize, and revealed the hot, violent cosmos.

    Illustration: NASA/CXC
  • Space geodesy

    Weighing and shaping the Earth

    At the dawn of the Space Age, our worldwide satellite-tracking network pioneered space geodesy, refining Earth's shape and gravity field, laying groundwork for modern GPS.

    Image: NASA

Where we come from

Over a century of looking up

We began on the National Mall with one man measuring sunlight, and grew into one of the largest astrophysics institutions on Earth.

1890

Born at the Smithsonian

Secretary Samuel Pierpont Langley founds the Smithsonian Astrophysical Observatory in a wooden shed behind the Castle in Washington, D.C., to study the Sun's energy, the "astro-physics" of its day.

1900s–1940s

Chasing the solar constant

Under Charles Greeley Abbot, we build solar-monitoring stations on mountaintops from Chile to California, making decades of measurements of how the Sun powers Earth's climate.

1955

The move to Cambridge

We relocate to Cambridge, Massachusetts, alongside Harvard College Observatory, and within two years are tracking the first artificial satellites at the dawn of the Space Age.

1973

A powerful partnership

SAO and Harvard College Observatory join forces as the Center for Astrophysics, uniting Smithsonian and Harvard scientists in a single research community.

1999

Chandra opens the X-ray sky

NASA's Chandra X-ray Observatory launches aboard Space Shuttle Columbia, with science operations led by us, and rewrites what we know about the high-energy universe.

2019

Seeing the unseeable

The Event Horizon Telescope, with our leadership, unveils the first image of a black hole, the glowing ring of M87*, seen around the world within hours.

Today

From Earth's air to cosmic dawn

TEMPO watches North America's air quality from orbit, the Giant Magellan Telescope rises in Chile, and our scientists probe everything from exoplanets to the first galaxies.
